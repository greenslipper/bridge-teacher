TIP 1 OF 21

Draw trumps as soon as possible — unless you need them for something

Rule: In a suit contract, your default should be to draw the opponents' trumps early.

Rationale: Every trump the opponents retain is a card that can unexpectedly beat one of your winners. Once their trumps are gone, your side-suit winners are safe from being ruffed.

Example: You're in 4♠ with:

  • You: ♠AKQJx
  • Dummy: ♠xxx

You have eight trumps. If you start cashing your excellent diamonds while opponents still have five spades between them, one may run out of diamonds and ruff a winner.

Instead, play ♠A, ♠K, ♠Q. If they're 3–2, all opposing trumps are gone. Now cash the diamonds safely.

Main exception: You need your trumps for ruffing, entries, a crossruff, etc. This leads directly to Rule 2.

TIP 2 OF 21

Ruff losers in the short-trump hand before drawing trumps

Rule: If you need dummy's trumps to ruff losers, do that before exhausting dummy's trumps.

Rationale: Ruffing in the short-trump hand usually creates extra tricks. Ruffing in your long-trump hand usually doesn't.

Suppose:

  • You: ♠AKQJx ♥xx
  • Dummy: ♠xxx ♥Axx

You have two heart losers.

If you immediately draw three rounds of trumps, dummy has no spades left. Your heart losers remain losers.

Instead, arrange to ruff one or both hearts in dummy, then draw trumps.

This gives a very useful question before drawing trumps:

Do I need dummy's trumps for anything?

If no → draw trumps.

If yes → do that thing first.

TIP 3 OF 21

Take essential discards before drawing trumps when necessary

This sounds contradictory to Rule 1, but isn't.

Rule: If you have a side-suit winner that can dispose of an immediate loser, sometimes cash it before drawing trumps.

Rationale: Drawing trumps may require giving the opponents the lead. If they can then cash your loser, you've missed your opportunity to discard it.

Example: You're in 4♠. Dummy has:

  • ♣A
  • ♦AKQ

You have a losing ♣K? Let's make it clearer: you have one losing heart, and dummy's ♦Q is an established winner on which you can throw it.

But opponents hold ♠A.

If you start drawing trumps, they win ♠A and may immediately cash the ♥A/K or whatever your vulnerable heart loser is.

Instead:

cash ♦Q → discard heart loser → then attack trumps.

The distinction is:

If the discard can wait safely, draw trumps first. If it can't, take the discard now.
TIP 4 OF 21

Ruffing in the long-trump hand usually doesn't gain a trick

This is worth understanding rather than merely memorising.

Suppose you have:

  • Hand: 5 spades
  • Dummy: 3 spades

You normally expect your five spades to produce roughly five trump tricks.

If you ruff something in your hand, you've used one of those five spades. You still tend to make five trump tricks total.

But if you ruff in dummy, you can make:

5 trump tricks from hand + 1 ruff in dummy = 6 tricks.

So:

Ruffing in the short hand creates extra trump tricks.

Exception: Ruffing in the long hand can still be necessary to prevent a loser, maintain control, set up a suit, etc. It just doesn't ordinarily increase your total trump tricks.

TIP 5 OF 21

Play high cards from the short side first

One of the most practically useful rules.

Suppose:

  • Hand: ♦Qx
  • Dummy: ♦AKxxx

You want five diamond tricks assuming a reasonable split.

Play Q first, then cross to A/K.

Rationale: You want to end up in the hand containing the long cards.

If you cash A-K first, then Q, you'll finish in the wrong hand and may have no way back to dummy's remaining diamonds.

The general rule is:

When cashing equivalent winners between two hands, preserve the entries to the hand containing the length.

That's the real rule; "high cards from the short side first" is the convenient shortcut.

TIP 6 OF 21

Preserve entries to your long suit

This is the broader concept behind Rule 5.

Suppose dummy has:

♦KQJ10x

and you have:

♦xx

The ♦A is outstanding.

You can easily establish four diamond winners by forcing out the ace.

But imagine dummy's only outside entry is ♣A.

If you casually cash ♣A early, then play diamonds and the opponents take ♦A, you might never get back to dummy.

Your four beautiful diamond winners are useless.

Instead:

attack diamonds → force out ♦A → retain ♣A → later use ♣A to reach the established diamonds.

So when you see a long suit, ask two questions:

How do I establish it?
How do I get back to it afterwards?

The second question is surprisingly easy to forget.

TIP 7 OF 21

Establish long suits early; cash winners late

Suppose in 3NT you have:

♠AK

♥AK

♦A

♣KQJ10x

That's seven immediate winners, but clubs can potentially give you another four.

You shouldn't necessarily cash ♠AK, ♥AK and ♦A first.

Instead, attack clubs immediately.

Why?

Because you still have controls in all the other suits. You can afford to lose the ♣A while opponents can't run anything against you.

After ♣A is gone, you have your established club winners plus your existing winners.

General NT strategy:

Identify the suit that will provide your missing tricks and work on it immediately.

Don't cash winners simply because they're winners.

TIP 8 OF 21

Lose unavoidable losers early, while you still have control

Related to the above.

Suppose you're in 3NT and have:

  • Hand: ♠Axx ♥AKx ♦KQJ10 ♣Ax
  • Dummy: ♠Kxx ♥Qxx ♦xxx ♣KQx

You need nine tricks. You have 7 immediate winners: two spades, three hearts and two clubs. Diamonds can provide the extra tricks you need, but the opponents hold ♦A.

You're therefore going to lose to ♦A eventually.

Attack diamonds now, while you still have aces/kings stopping the opponents' other suits. Once ♦A is gone, your remaining high diamonds are established.

If instead you first cash all seven of your immediate winners and then play diamonds, an opponent wins ♦A after you've used up all your controls. They may now be able to cash several tricks before you regain the lead.

The idea is:

If you're inevitably going to give them the lead, choose when to do it.

Often earlier is safer.

TIP 9 OF 21

Don't automatically cash your aces

Beginners see:

A K Q

and think "three tricks."

Strong players also think:

controls and entries.

Suppose you're in 3NT. West leads a spade, and between your hand and dummy you have:

  • Hand: ♠Axx
  • Dummy: ♠xx

Suppose the bidding and opening lead suggest West started with five spades and East with three.

If you take ♠A on the first round, West still has four spades. If East later gains the lead, East can return a spade and West may be able to cash the rest of the suit.

Instead, you can sometimes duck the first two rounds and take ♠A on the third:

duck → duck → take ♠A.

By then East has played all three of their original spades. If East later gains the lead, they have no spade left to return to West. You've broken the defenders' communication.

That's the hold-up play.

Likewise, an ace in dummy might be your only entry to a long suit, so cashing it too early can strand your winners.

For example, dummy has ♦KQJ10x and ♣A, and ♣A is your only entry to dummy. If you cash ♣A before forcing out ♦A, you may later establish the diamonds but have no way back to dummy to cash them. Keep ♣A as the entry until the diamonds are established.

So:

An ace isn't merely a winner. It's control over when that suit can be run.
TIP 10 OF 21

Lead towards honours, not away from them

Suppose:

  • Hand: ♥AQ
  • Dummy: ♥xx

and ♥K is missing.

Lead a small heart from dummy towards AQ.

If the opponent before AQ plays low, play Q.

If K is favourably placed, Q wins.

If instead you lead Q away from AQ, the opponent holding K after you simply covers it. You've gained nothing.

Another example:

Kx opposite xx

Lead small towards K.

If the ace is before your king, they have an unpleasant choice: play A and establish your K, or play low and let your K win.

General principle:

Unsupported honours like having small cards led towards them.
TIP 11 OF 21

Don't take a finesse you don't need

Suppose you're in 4♠ and need ten tricks.

You count 10 certain tricks.

You also hold:

♥AQ

with K missing.

Taking the finesse might produce an eleventh trick.

But if it loses, perhaps the opponents can cash three more tricks.

Why take the risk?

Take your ten winners and make the contract.

This sounds obvious, but it's an important bridge mindset:

The objective isn't to win the maximum conceivable number of tricks. It's to make the contract as reliably as possible.

In duplicate, overtricks matter, so the calculation can change—but making the contract remains enormously important.

TIP 12 OF 21

Try your chances in the right order

Suppose you need one additional trick.

You have:

♣AKxx opposite ♣xxx

and

♥AQ opposite ♥xx

You could immediately take the 50% heart finesse.

But perhaps clubs splitting 3–3 will also provide your ninth trick. You and dummy have seven clubs, so the opponents have six. If those six are split 3–3, each opponent must follow to the first three rounds of clubs. After you play ♣A, ♣K and then a third club, all six opposing clubs — including ♣Q and ♣J wherever they started — have been forced out because the defenders must follow suit if they can. Your fourth club is then a winner.

If you can safely cash ♣A-K and test clubs without compromising the heart finesse, do that first.

If clubs break → great, no finesse required.

If not → take the heart finesse.

You've effectively given yourself two chances rather than one.

General principle:

Try chances that leave other chances available before taking an all-or-nothing line.

This becomes a huge part of more advanced declarer play.

TIP 13 OF 21

Count trumps every hand

You and dummy have eight spades.

Therefore opponents have five.

First round: both follow.

3 remain.

Second round: both follow.

1 remains.

Don't think "I played two rounds of trumps."

Think:

One opposing trump remains.

Now decide whether it matters.

If you have loads of side-suit winners, it probably does: draw it.

If drawing it would consume your last trump in dummy and you need that trump for a ruff, perhaps leave it outstanding temporarily.

TIP 14 OF 21

When someone shows out, stop and reconstruct

You have eight hearts. Opponents therefore have five.

You play ♥A:

East follows, West follows.

You play ♥K:

East follows, West discards.

You now know opponents started:

West = 1 heart

East = 4 hearts

Not approximately. Exactly.

That information also tells you about the other suits. If West has already shown six diamonds and one heart, you know 7 of West's 13 cards, leaving only six spaces for clubs and spades.

This is how counting gradually turns into knowing opponents' hands.

TIP 15 OF 21

Second hand low

Now defence.

Suppose hearts are played like this:

  • Declarer leads: ♥x
  • You, second to play: ♥Kxx
  • Dummy: ♥AQx

Usually play low.

Why?

If declarer plays dummy's Q, your K remains above it and can win later.

If you unnecessarily play K immediately, dummy plays A and Q becomes established. You've spent your K without gaining anything.

More generally, playing an honour second-hand can unnecessarily sacrifice it before declarer has committed an honour.

Hence:

Second hand low.

It's a default, not a law.

TIP 16 OF 21

Third hand high — more precisely, play the highest card necessary

Now suppose partner leads a small heart and dummy plays low:

  • Partner: ♥Q10xx (leads a small heart)
  • Dummy: ♥xxx
  • You, third to play: ♥KJx
  • Declarer: holds ♥A among their hearts

Usually play ♥K.

Why?

Partner has attacked this suit and you're trying to prevent declarer winning cheaply. The play may go:

partner's small heart → dummy low → your ♥K → declarer's ♥A

Your K has forced out declarer's ace. Partner is still left with ♥Q10x, so your side is now much closer to establishing heart winners when it regains the lead.

If you casually play low instead, declarer may be able to win the trick with a lower card while keeping ♥A for later.

Hence the useful beginner rule:

Second hand low; third hand high.

More precisely, third hand should usually play the highest card necessary to force declarer's higher card. As you learn more, there are situations with sequences of honours where that won't literally mean playing your highest card.

These two rules work together: second hand generally waits; third hand generally tries to force declarer's higher card.

TIP 17 OF 21

Cover an honour with an honour — when it promotes something

Suppose hearts are arranged like this:

  • Declarer: ♥Q10...
  • You: ♥Kx
  • Dummy: ♥Axx
  • Partner: ♥J9x

Declarer leads ♥Q.

If you cover:

♥Q → ♥K → ♥A

it looks as though you've wasted your king. But the Q and K have both disappeared. Partner's ♥J is now above declarer's remaining ♥10, so your sacrifice of the king has promoted partner's jack into a potential winner.

If you don't cover, declarer's ♥Q can win the first trick while declarer still retains ♥10 and dummy still retains ♥A. Your king has not forced either of those cards out.

The real rule isn't "always cover an honour."

It's:

Cover when doing so can promote a card for your side.

With sequences, particularly, you sometimes wait until the last honour is played.

TIP 18 OF 21

Don't give declarer unnecessary ruffs

Suppose you're defending 4♠. Hearts have been played and you discover:

  • Declarer: no hearts left
  • Dummy: still has spade trumps
  • Your side: still holds high hearts that would normally be winners

If you lead another heart, declarer simply ruffs it with a spade. Your high heart isn't a useful winner while declarer is void and still has trumps.

So switch to another suit rather than giving declarer a free ruff.

Related application: sometimes defenders should lead trumps.

Suppose instead dummy has:

  • ♠876
  • ♥4

Dummy has three trumps and a singleton heart, while declarer appears to have several losing hearts. Declarer's plan may be to ruff those heart losers with dummy's small spades.

If your side gains the lead and plays a trump, you remove one of dummy's potential ruffs. If you get in again and play another trump, you may remove most or all of dummy's ruffing power before declarer can use it.

If dummy's small trumps are clearly going to be used to ruff declarer's losers, consider leading trumps yourself.
TIP 19 OF 21

Cash defensive winners when they're about to disappear

You're defending 4♠.

Suppose:

  • Declarer: ♥K3
  • You: ♥A7
  • Dummy: has established diamond winners, say ♦AKQ...

At the moment, declarer's ♥3 is a heart loser that your side can potentially cash. But if declarer gets to dummy and starts cashing diamonds, they can discard ♥3 on one of those winners.

Your ♥A will still be an ace, but the heart loser you hoped to capture has disappeared. So if you have the opportunity and can see the discard coming, you may need to take the heart trick now.

Ask:

"If I don't take this trick now, could declarer throw the loser away?"

If yes, cash it now.

But if dummy has no source of discards and your ace isn't going anywhere, don't panic. Keeping it may be better.

So defence involves distinguishing:

active defence: take/create tricks urgently because they may disappear.

from

passive defence: don't give declarer anything they couldn't make themselves.

TIP 20 OF 21

Use the auction as part of card play

This is perhaps the most important "advanced beginner → good player" transition.

Suppose opponent opened 1NT (15–17).

Dummy comes down with 8 HCP.

You have 10 HCP.

That's already:

15–17 + 8 + 10 = 33–35 HCP accounted for.

Your partner therefore has only 5–7 HCP.

That radically constrains where missing honours can be.

Likewise:

Opponent opened 1♠ → probably 5+ spades.

Later they've shown 4 hearts.

You already know at least nine of their thirteen cards.

The auction doesn't finish when the opening lead is made. It's evidence you should continue using for all 13 tricks.

TIP 21 OF 21

Return partner's suit — unless you have a reason not to

Rule: If partner leads a suit and you later gain the lead, returning that suit is often the right default.

Rationale: Partner's opening lead often means they're trying to establish tricks in that suit. Your job may be to help them continue that plan rather than start a completely new suit.

Example: Partner leads a small heart against 3NT.

  • Partner: ♥Q10xxx
  • Dummy: ♥xx
  • You: ♥Kxx
  • Declarer: ♥Axx

On the first trick, you play ♥K and declarer wins ♥A. Later you gain the lead in another suit.

Now return a heart.

Declarer's ace is already gone, and partner still has ♥Q10xx. Returning the suit helps partner establish and eventually cash those hearts. If you switch to an unrelated suit instead, you may give declarer the time they need to make their contract.

This is a default, not an absolute rule. The auction, dummy, and cards already played may give you a good reason to switch.

Partner's lead is information: if they appear to be establishing a suit, help them finish the job.

Quick reference

If you want these to become automatic, I'd memorise this sequence:

Before playing:

Count winners in NT; count losers in suits → identify where extra tricks/discards come from → identify entries.

In a trump contract:

Draw trumps unless you need them → ruff losers in short hand → take urgent discards → then draw trumps → cash established winners.

In NT:

Identify your source of tricks → establish it early → preserve entries → don't cash controls unnecessarily → lose necessary tricks while you still control other suits.

Technique:

High cards from short side first → lead towards honours → don't take unnecessary finesses → combine your chances → count every suit.

Defence:

Second hand low → third hand high → cover honours when it promotes → return partner's suit unless there's a reason to switch → use the auction → count declarer's tricks → decide whether you need active or passive defence.

Those ~20 principles will do far more for your actual results at this stage than learning another twenty bidding conventions.
